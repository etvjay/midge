01 · The problem

Deciding together with money on the line is hard

A fund splitting a pot, a DAO picking a quote, a team of AI agents buying together. Today there are three ways to do it, and each one breaks something.

Do it in public

Everyone sees your bids before they happen, and trades against you.

Trust an organizer

One person holds the money. If they vanish or get hacked, it is gone.

Trust a black box

Secure hardware keeps things private, but if it can pay out anything, one bug empties the pot.

Midge gives you all three at once: decisions stay private, no one holds the money, and no one can lock it up.

02 · How it works

Every group gets a room, rules and an exit

Every group on Midge is built from the same three things.

123

1 · The room

Members decide in private

Each member seals their bid so only the room can open it. The room adds them up and lets out just the result. Even the people running the machine see nothing.

A room is run by a host: secure hardware that proves which code it is running. There are two kinds:

For more safety a room can run on two independent hosts, and both must sign before any money moves.

2 · The rules

The rules are public and can't change

When the group starts, its rules go into a contract on NEAR: who can be paid, how much, and until when. Everyone agrees to them when they put money in.

One limit to know: "how much" caps each payment as a share of what the group holds at that moment. We measured a stand-in for a compromised host making three payments in a row, each within the limit, and releasing 58% of a group against a stated 25%. Pacing the payments is not yet enforced.

Any payment that breaks the rules is refused, even if the room approved it.

3 · The exit

Anyone can leave with their share

If the room stops working, a timer starts. When it runs out, any member can take back exactly what they put in, on their own, without asking anyone.

One condition: the exit waits while a payout is being finished, which normally takes seconds. If a payout ever got stuck, anyone could finish it after 100 blocks, about two minutes, and the exit would open. That recovery is tested in the contract and has never been needed on testnet.

03 · Who uses it

Any group, on one shared network

People, DAOs, companies and AI agents all join the same way, with a NEAR account. One contract on NEAR holds every group, so groups are easy to find, and one member can be in several at once.

…groups on the network right now

Auctions and token sales

Bidders send sealed bids. The rules fix the winner and the price, and everyone else gets their deposit back.

Investing together

Backers agree on a round and invest from one shared address.

Lending

Lenders send sealed offers. The best terms win, and the others stay private.

Big trades

Two desks swap a large order without showing its size to the market.

Voting

Members cast sealed votes. Only the result is published, and it decides a payment.

Paying people

A team or a set of contractors is paid from one pool. Who each payment goes to can be hidden behind one-time addresses, and each person gets a private receipt. The amounts are public on NEAR.

AI agents together

A team of agents buys or pools money, each holding a key that can do one thing only.

Shared AI

Members pool credits and run a private request on an AI model.

A group's room can be one-off (Flash: one task, then it closes) or ongoing (Dark Vault: many rounds). What has run on testnet: investing together, sealed auctions, lending quotes, requests for quote in both directions, block trades, voting and paying people, each next to a public version. The shared-AI example is a simulation. Paying people has also run, next to a public pay run: it hides who is paid and gives each person a private receipt, not the amounts, and with distinct unchanged salaries an observer can still link a person across months by amount. Investing together was compared with a public round and a trusted organizer: both of those show every backer's amount, and the organizer can walk off with the money. Midge hides the amounts only when everyone puts in the same deposit and pays an equal share; if each backer pays their own amount, the amounts become public when the round settles. Sealed auctions were compared with a public one: in the public auction a watcher who bids at the last moment can take the lot from the bidder who values it most, and sealed bids prevent that. They still publish the winner and the price, and they need every bidder to lock the same deposit. Lending quotes and block trades were compared the same way: in an open quote round a watcher who quotes at the last moment can win the loan from the lowest-cost lender, and an open order book shows every desk's size and limit while sealed orders show neither; the sealed versions cost more gas and need every participant to lock a deposit. Only the cash moves: nothing on this chain enforces repayment or delivers the asset.

04 · AI agents

A hijacked AI agent still can't take the money

An agent can be given a key that does one thing only: take its owner's money back out. We tricked such an agent into trying to send everything to a thief. Every attempt was refused.

Agents connect through MCP tools, an SDK or an HTTP API. The key limits what a fooled agent can do; it does not stop the agent from being fooled.

Private work in secure hardware. Final say on NEAR

    

      

        
Three systems work together, and each one checks the one before it.

        

          
NEAR AI Cloud
An AI model helps the group decide, for example how to split the money. It only sees the total, never anyone's bid.

          
Our program on Phala
Opens the sealed bids, adds them up exactly, and signs the result only if it fits.

          
NEAR
The contract pays out only within the rules, and runs the exit.
